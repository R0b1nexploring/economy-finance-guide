People, Flows & Balance Sheets

Follow the people and the payments.

Your mission

Follow a payment through the economy without losing track of who owns an asset, who owes a debt, and who receives income. This is the starting point for every later region of the map.

The five main groups

A household works, buys goods and services, saves, borrows, pays taxes, and owns assets. A business organizes production, hires people, sells output, and finances equipment and operations. Government collects revenue, provides public services, makes transfers, borrows, and sets rules. Financial institutions handle payments, extend credit, pool savings, insure risks, and help people trade financial claims. The rest of the world buys and sells goods, services, and assets across borders. A central bank belongs on this map too, but has a special role in the monetary and financial system.

The same person can occupy several roles. You may be a worker, a consumer, a depositor, a borrower, and a shareholder. A policy can help one of these roles while making another more expensive. A higher interest rate may increase some savings income and increase the cost of a variable-rate loan at the same time.

A flow is measured over time; a stock is measured at a moment

Your monthly salary is a flow. Your bank balance today is a stock. A government's annual deficit is a flow; outstanding public debt is a stock. A company's annual profit is a flow; its accumulated equity is a stock. Always ask, "Per month or year, or at a particular date?"

A balance sheet lists assets and liabilities at a moment in time. An asset is something owned or a claim with economic value. A liability is an obligation. Net worth, also called equity in a business, is the residual after liabilities are subtracted from assets.

For a household with a bank deposit of 8,000, an apartment worth 200,000, and a mortgage of 150,000, the simplified balance sheet is:

Item Amount in hypothetical currency units
Bank deposit 8,000
Apartment 200,000
Total assets 208,000
Mortgage liability 150,000
Net worth 58,000

An increase in the apartment's estimated value can raise net worth without producing cash to pay this month's bills. An asset sale can produce cash without creating new production. These distinctions will matter when you study credit, asset prices, and crises.

Follow one salary

Original hypothetical example. A business receives 100,000 in monthly sales. It spends 40,000 on intermediate inputs, 35,000 on wages, 10,000 on rent and other operating costs, and has 15,000 remaining before financing costs, taxes, and any depreciation not already counted. That remaining amount is not necessarily final accounting profit, and it is not automatically cash available to shareholders.

One worker receives 3,000 after relevant payroll deductions. They spend 1,800, make a 400 loan payment, and retain 800 in their bank account. The consumer spending becomes revenue somewhere else. The loan payment must be split between principal and interest: principal reduces a liability, while interest is the lender's income and the borrower's cost. The retained deposit is a household financial asset and a bank liability.

This small story connects all five groups. Some inputs may be imported. Some revenue goes to taxes. The company may borrow to pay suppliers before customers pay it. The household may save through a pension fund. The economy is a network of payments, production, and claims.

The map is not a fixed pile of money

Financial assets often have a counterpart: your deposit is the bank's liability, a bond is the issuer's debt, and a share is an ownership claim. Real assets such as buildings and machinery are different. Production and innovation can increase real output; exchanging claims can redistribute ownership and risk. Neither every transaction nor every gain represents an equal loss in real wealth for someone else.

Use the Red Alert analogy to keep track of locations and connections. Real people have different information and objectives, economic rules can change, and outcomes are uncertain. Revealing a mechanism helps you ask better questions; it does not reveal the future.

Check your understanding

  1. Your deposit rises by 10,000 because you take out a 10,000 loan. Has your net worth immediately increased by 10,000?
  2. Is an annual government deficit the same thing as the government's debt?
  3. Can a household have positive net worth and still struggle to pay a bill?

Answer key

  1. No. An asset and a liability rise together, before fees or other changes.
  2. No. A deficit measures a gap over a period; debt measures an outstanding stock. Other transactions and valuation changes can also affect debt.
  3. Yes. Valuable assets can be difficult or slow to turn into spendable money. Wealth and payment capacity are related but distinct.

Resources, Production & Income

Find the real resources behind the financial claims.

Your mission

Understand what the financial system ultimately connects to: people producing useful goods and services, receiving income, and deciding how to use it.

What counts as a resource?

Production uses labor, land, energy, materials, machines, infrastructure, knowledge, and time. Capital here means produced assets used in production, such as equipment and software. Later, "bank capital" will mean a financial loss-absorbing buffer; the same word has different meanings in different contexts.

Productivity measures output relative to inputs. A better machine, better organization, improved skills, or better software can allow more output per hour. Higher productivity can make higher incomes possible, but how the gains are distributed also depends on bargaining, competition, institutions, ownership, and prices. A worker's wage is not a direct, perfect measurement of their social value.

A small workshop

Original hypothetical example. A workshop sells 100 stools at 20 each. Revenue is 2,000. Materials purchased from other businesses cost 800. The workshop's value added is 1,200. Suppose wages are 700 and equipment depreciation is 100. Gross operating surplus is 500 before depreciation; the remaining 400 after depreciation is net operating surplus in this simplified example. These are different ways the workshop's added value is allocated.

If an improved process allows the same labor hours to produce 120 stools, potential output rises. Actual sales might still remain at 100 if demand is weak. Prices might fall if competitors also improve. Wages might rise, profits might rise, or the gain might be shared. "Can produce more" and "will sell more" are different claims.

GDP: production, income, and expenditure

Gross domestic product measures production within an economy over a period. It can be viewed through the value added in production, the income generated, or the final expenditure on the output. Adding intermediate purchases and final sales together would count the same production repeatedly. Statistical estimates across the approaches can differ because measurement is imperfect. BEA: the expenditure approach to GDP.

The familiar expenditure identity is GDP = consumption + investment + government purchases + exports − imports. "Investment" in this identity means items such as new equipment, structures, and inventories; buying an existing share is a financial transaction. Imports are subtracted because imported content may already appear in the other spending categories. The subtraction isolates domestic production; it does not establish that imports are economically harmful. BEA: measuring the economy.

GDP is useful, but it is not a complete score for a society. It does not by itself tell you who receives the income, whether the environment is improving, how much unpaid work is done, or whether an expansion is sustainable. GDP, household purchasing power, company profits, and stock prices can move differently.

Supply, demand, and adjustment

Demand concerns what buyers are willing and able to purchase at different prices. Supply concerns what sellers are willing and able to offer. A change in income, tastes, production costs, or technology can shift those relationships. Price and quantity outcomes depend on how readily buyers and suppliers adjust. Adjustment is often slow when capacity, housing, energy systems, or skilled labor cannot change quickly. IMF: supply and demand.

If a café gains customers while its tables and staff remain fixed, it may first use idle capacity, then hire more staff, then raise prices or turn customers away. If electricity becomes more expensive, the same café faces a cost shock even without stronger demand. These cases can produce different combinations of output, prices, and profit. You will return to them when studying inflation and monetary policy.

Saving and investment connect today to tomorrow

Saving is income not used for current consumption. Real investment creates or improves productive capacity. Financial institutions and markets help connect financing decisions to spending on those assets, but the connection is not mechanical. A firm invests when expected sales, financing conditions, uncertainty, and capacity needs make the project worthwhile. Credit can finance productive investment or purchases of existing assets; those uses have different economic consequences.

Check your understanding

  1. Why is the workshop's value added 1,200 rather than 2,000?
  2. Does buying an existing company share count as new factory investment in GDP?
  3. Does higher productivity guarantee that every worker's wage rises immediately?

Answer key

  1. The materials contain value produced elsewhere. Subtracting intermediate inputs avoids double counting.
  2. No. The share is a financial ownership claim. A company's subsequent purchase of new productive equipment is a separate transaction.
  3. No. Demand, bargaining, competition, institutions, and distribution affect who receives the gain and when.

Money & Credit

Understand how payments and borrowing work.

Balance-sheet explorer

Borrower's balance sheet

Bank's balance sheet

Map region: the payment network and the agreements that let people use resources today in exchange for future payments.

Your mission: explain what the money in a bank account represents; follow a loan from creation to repayment; distinguish interest, liquidity, capital, and leverage; and identify the conditions under which borrowing improves or weakens a household or business.

All amounts below are hypothetical currency units, written as CU. They are teaching examples, not descriptions of a current country or market.

3.1 Money is useful because other people accept it

Imagine an engineer trying to exchange two hours of work for groceries. The grocer might not need engineering work today. A widely accepted means of payment allows the engineer to sell work to one person and buy food from another.

Money performs three familiar jobs: a medium of exchange for payments, a unit of account for quoting prices and recording debts, and a store of value that carries purchasing power across time. The last job can work imperfectly: a balance of 1,000 CU remains 1,000 CU even if the things it buys become more expensive.

Keep three objects separate. Cash is physical money. Bank deposits are account balances usable for payments and claims on the bank. Central bank reserves are balances eligible institutions hold at the central bank, used for settlement and monetary operations. A household normally pays a shop with its deposit; its bank may use reserves to settle with the shop's bank. The Bank of England describes this distinction for the UK; the institutions and definitions used in monetary statistics vary by country. Bank of England: How is money created?

In the Red Alert analogy, the payment system is the network that allows supplies to move. The actual supplies are workers' time, food, electricity, machines, and knowledge. More payment capacity does not, by itself, produce more supplies.

3.2 A bank deposit is somebody else's liability

A balance sheet records what an entity owns and owes at a particular moment:

Assets − liabilities = equity, or net worth.

Consider a household with 12,000 CU in deposits, a vehicle worth 8,000 CU, and a 5,000 CU loan. Its assets total 20,000 CU and its net worth is 15,000 CU. Its monthly salary is a flow of income, not another asset to add in full to that snapshot.

Now change viewpoint. The household's 12,000 CU deposit is an asset to the household and a liability to its bank: the bank must honor permitted withdrawals and transfers. The household's loan is its liability and the bank's asset: the bank has a contractual right to future payments.

This paired view stops a common mistake. Adding up every person's financial claims without subtracting the corresponding obligations can make the same underlying relationship look like newly created net wealth.

3.3 Follow a bank loan through four steps

Suppose a bakery borrows 50,000 CU from Bank A to purchase an oven.

Step 1 — origination. Bank A records a 50,000 CU loan asset and credits a 50,000 CU deposit to the bakery. The bakery gains a deposit asset and an equal debt liability. In this simplified transaction, bank deposit money increases, while the bakery's net worth does not jump by 50,000 CU. Commercial-bank lending commonly creates deposits in this way; it is not simply a mechanical redistribution of previously deposited cash. Bank of England: Money creation in the modern economy

Step 2 — purchase. The bakery pays the oven supplier, which uses Bank B. The bakery's deposit decreases and the supplier's deposit increases. Bank A must settle the payment with Bank B, typically by transferring central bank money through the relevant settlement arrangements. Bank A therefore needs usable settlement funds or a way to obtain them. Writing a loan into its accounts does not remove that need.

Step 3 — production. The bakery now has an oven instead of the deposit, while its debt remains. Whether the loan was economically useful depends on what happens next. Perhaps the oven reduces energy use, allows more sales, or breaks down. Financial accounting records the transaction; business conditions determine its success.

Step 4 — repayment. Suppose the bakery later pays 10,000 CU of loan principal from a deposit at its lending bank. The deposit liability and outstanding loan asset both fall by 10,000 CU. Repayment of bank-loan principal contracts deposit money in the simplified banking system. Interest is different: it is income to the bank, and its effects depend on how the bank spends, distributes, or retains that income. A default is also different from repayment: writing off a bad loan reduces the bank's assets and capital; it does not automatically erase a deposit that the borrower already paid to someone else. Bank of England: How is money created?

If an ordinary person lends another person 500 CU by bank transfer, the lender's deposit falls and the borrower's rises. Total deposits are unchanged by that transfer. Credit can exist without creating additional bank money. Bank of England: New forms of digital money, Box A

3.4 Why banks cannot lend without limits

The bank needs creditworthy customers, acceptable expected returns, settlement liquidity, and compliance with capital and other regulatory requirements. Creating a matching deposit is the accounting mechanism; it is not a guarantee that the loan can be funded profitably or repaid.

Liquidity concerns meeting payments when due. Capital is a loss-absorbing cushion. The two are related but different. Suppose a simplified bank has assets worth 1,000 CU, liabilities of 920 CU, and equity of 80 CU. A 30 CU asset loss reduces equity to 50 CU. Giving the bank a short-term loan can help it make payments, but it also adds an obligation; it does not simply restore the lost equity. Bank capital requirements are tied to risks and can include multiple buffers. ECB Banking Supervision: Why do banks need capital?

For an everyday analogy, a homeowner can have positive net worth yet lack cash for tomorrow's bill. Conversely, someone can have plenty of cash today but owe more than all their assets are worth. A bank can also encounter either problem.

3.5 Interest is a price with several ingredients

Principal is the amount borrowed. Interest is the charge for using it. Maturity is when payment becomes due. Collateral is an asset pledged to support repayment. A fixed rate remains unchanged under the contract; a floating rate can reset according to its terms.

A 10,000 CU interest-only loan at 6% costs 600 CU in annual interest while the principal remains outstanding. A fully amortizing loan repays principal gradually, so its payment calculation differs. A quoted rate alone does not tell you the total cost: fees, compounding, repayment timing, and contract terms matter.

A lending rate can reflect the underlying funding rate, expected default losses, operating costs, capital costs, and the lender's margin. If a policy rate falls by one percentage point but a borrower's perceived risk rises, that borrower's offered rate may still increase.

Distinguish nominal from real values. Nominal amounts are stated in currency; real amounts adjust for purchasing power. The common approximation is real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation. For decisions before inflation is known, expected inflation is the relevant estimate. ECB: Nominal and real interest rates

Original example: saving 1,000 CU at 4% gives 1,040 CU after one year. If the price of a representative basket rises 3%, the exact purchasing-power gain is 1.04 ÷ 1.03 − 1, or about 0.97%. If the basket rises 7%, the same deposit has a real loss of about 2.80%. A negative real rate does not cancel the nominal payment required by a loan contract.

3.6 Leverage magnifies changes in the owner's stake

Leverage means using borrowed funds or other obligations to increase exposure relative to equity.

Consider two hypothetical owners of identical 100,000 CU productive assets. One pays entirely with equity. The other contributes 20,000 CU and borrows 80,000 CU. Before interest and fees:

Asset value after a change Unleveraged owner's equity Leveraged owner's equity
110,000 CU 110,000 CU: a 10% gain 30,000 CU: a 50% gain
90,000 CU 90,000 CU: a 10% loss 10,000 CU: a 50% loss
80,000 CU 80,000 CU: a 20% loss 0 CU: equity exhausted

The debt remains 80,000 CU in this example. Interest makes the actual outcome less favorable, and loan terms may force a sale before the owner chooses one. Real recovery and personal liability depend on the contract and local law.

Borrowing can bring spending forward. It also assigns part of future cash flow to debt service. A bakery expecting 15,000 CU in annual cash available for debt payments might manage a 6,000 CU obligation. If that cash falls to 4,000 CU, a profitable business over several years could still face an immediate payment problem. Examine the amount, timing, and reliability of cash flows, not just an optimistic long-term average.

3.7 Misconceptions to clear from the map

  • “Every loan creates new money.” A commercial-bank loan can create a deposit; an ordinary transfer-funded loan reallocates existing deposits.
  • “If banks create deposits, deposits and funding do not matter.” Payments can leave a bank; it must obtain settlement liquidity and manage funding costs and risks.
  • “A loan makes the borrower richer immediately.” It initially creates an asset and an obligation. Wealth depends on what the resources achieve afterward.
  • “Inflation makes every debt easy to repay.” Nominal debt service still requires cash; income may not rise with prices, and floating interest rates may reset.

3.8 Check yourself

Question 1. Your assets are 40,000 CU and debts are 15,000 CU. You borrow 5,000 CU and keep it in your account. What happens to assets, debt, and net worth?

Reveal answer

Answer. Assets rise to 45,000 CU, debt rises to 20,000 CU, and net worth remains 25,000 CU before fees. Your liquidity changes; your net worth does not rise automatically.

Question 2. A bank grants a 20,000 CU loan, and the borrower pays a supplier at another bank. Does the deposit disappear from the banking system?

Reveal answer

Answer. No. It moves to the supplier's bank, while the originating bank has a settlement obligation. Spending and principal repayment are different operations.

Question 3. An asset costs 60,000 CU, financed with 15,000 CU equity and 45,000 CU debt. It falls to 54,000 CU. What is the owner's equity loss before interest?

Reveal answer

Answer. Equity falls from 15,000 CU to 9,000 CU, a 40% loss. The asset fell 10%, but the owner's smaller equity stake absorbed the whole 6,000 CU decline.

Government & Central Banks

Trace a policy from its instrument to its effects.

Map region: public spending, taxation, borrowing, monetary policy, and the institutions that shape financial conditions.

Your mission: distinguish fiscal from monetary policy; follow how a rate change reaches households and firms; read a deficit without confusing it with debt; and explain why policy effects depend on economic conditions.

4.1 Two policy systems, different instruments

Fiscal policy concerns government spending, taxes, and transfers. Governments can purchase public services and infrastructure, collect revenue, and redistribute income. Monetary policy concerns financial conditions, especially interest rates and central bank operations. Its aims and legal framework differ across countries.

The Federal Reserve's US mandate includes maximum employment and stable prices. The ECB's primary objective is price stability for the euro area. Central bank independence means a particular institutional arrangement for pursuing legally assigned objectives, not unlimited authority over every part of the economy. Federal Reserve: The Fed Explained, monetary policy, ECB: Benefits of price stability

In the game analogy, fiscal choices allocate resources to public projects and services; monetary choices influence the financing conditions under which many participants act. Policymakers face incomplete information, competing objectives, and implementation delays.

4.2 Read a government budget as flows and stocks

Suppose a government receives 900 million CU in revenue during a year. It spends 950 million on programs and 50 million on interest.

  • Total expenditure is 1,000 million CU.
  • The overall deficit is 100 million CU.
  • The primary deficit, excluding interest, is 50 million CU.
  • If opening debt is 2,000 million CU and the government finances the deficit entirely by borrowing, debt ends at 2,100 million CU, assuming no other debt adjustments.

The deficit is a flow over a period; debt is an outstanding stock. The primary balance separates current revenue and non-interest spending from interest due on existing debt. Real government accounts can also include cash balances, asset transactions, valuation changes, and other adjustments, so changes in debt need not exactly equal the reported deficit. IMF: Public Finances in Modern History, IMF: Basic Debt Dynamics

Taxation also affects incentives and distribution. For example, an income tax affects take-home pay; a consumption tax affects purchase prices; different allowances and tax bases change who actually pays. The person legally responsible for remitting a tax need not bear its entire economic cost: prices, wages, or profits can adjust.

4.3 Fiscal policy can support demand and productive capacity

During a downturn, tax receipts can fall and certain benefit payments can rise without a new policy decision. These automatic stabilizers partly cushion private incomes. A government can also make a discretionary change, such as a temporary transfer or a new transport project. Spending and taxation influence total demand, while infrastructure and education can also affect future productive capacity. IMF: Fiscal Policy—Taking and Giving Away

Original teaching model: a town pays 1 million CU for additional repairs using otherwise idle local workers and equipment. Recipients spend 60% of each additional round of income on locally produced output. If this pattern repeated indefinitely, with no capacity limits or other responses, the arithmetic would be 1 + 0.6 + 0.36 + … = 2.5 million CU of additional output.

That 2.5 multiplier is a property of the assumptions, not a forecast. Some spending may go to imports. Taxes and saving interrupt subsequent rounds. Contractors may already be fully booked, so new public demand raises prices or displaces another project. Interest rates and private confidence may change. The relevant question is which workers, machines, materials, and financing were available, and what other spending responds.

Transfers are not themselves purchases of newly produced output in the GDP expenditure identity. Their contribution to demand depends on recipients' subsequent spending. Likewise, public borrowing creates a financing obligation; the economic value of a project depends on its costs and benefits.

4.4 Follow the interest-rate transmission chain

A central bank changes its policy setting. That influences market rates and funding costs. Banks and markets adjust lending and saving terms. Households and businesses reconsider spending, production, and hiring. Demand relative to productive capacity changes, influencing inflation. Expectations and exchange rates can provide additional channels. Transmission is indirect and takes time. Federal Reserve: How monetary policy affects inflation and employment

Original example: a company has 1 million CU of floating-rate debt. If its contractual rate falls from 7% to 6%, annual interest falls from 70,000 to 60,000 CU while principal is unchanged. The company might use the 10,000 CU difference for hiring, debt repayment, or additional cash. A fixed-rate borrower sees no immediate contractual change. A saver whose deposit rate falls receives less interest.

Transmission depends on pass-through. Suppose the policy rate falls by one percentage point, but banks become worried about defaults and increase their risk margin by the same amount. Some borrowers may see little change in offered rates or find that loans remain unavailable. If customers are disappearing, a cheaper loan may not persuade a business to buy a new machine.

Similarly, higher rates can discourage interest-sensitive demand, but they cannot directly grow crops or repair an interrupted energy supply. Policymakers must judge whether an inflation shock is likely to fade, spread into wages and other prices, or change expectations. A given action can impose different costs on borrowers, savers, workers, and sectors.

4.5 Reserve requirements, asset purchases, and liquidity support

Reserve requirements specify certain reserve holdings relative to defined liabilities where that framework applies. They are distinct from capital requirements. Their role differs by jurisdiction: the Federal Reserve reduced US reserve requirement ratios to zero in March 2020; the PBC's published policy framework includes required reserve ratios among its tools. These historical institutional examples illustrate why one country's textbook multiplier cannot automatically describe another country's operating system. Federal Reserve: Reserve requirements, PBC: China's Monetary Policy Framework, December 2018

Hypothetical illustration: on an unchanged eligible deposit base of 100 million CU, lowering a reserve ratio from 10% to 8% reduces the required amount by 2 million CU. It changes a regulatory constraint. It does not prove that exactly 2 million CU will be lent, or that lending will rise by a fixed multiple. Loan demand, capital, risk, and the rest of the policy framework still matter.

Quantitative easing, or QE, uses central bank asset purchases financed with central bank money to influence financial conditions. Purchases can affect longer-term yields and investors' portfolio choices. If a central bank buys a bond from a non-bank investor, the investor exchanges the bond for a deposit and the banking system receives reserves. If the seller is a bank, the bank exchanges an asset for reserves. QE is an asset transaction; it does not guarantee equivalent new spending or bank lending. Quantitative tightening reduces holdings through repayments or sales under the program's terms. Bank of England: Quantitative easing

Liquidity support supplies temporary financing to eligible institutions under relevant rules. Whether and how public authorities resolve an insolvent institution is a separate institutional question. Always identify the actual instrument and recipient before labeling every intervention “money printing.”

4.6 Debt sustainability is a path, not one number

A government owing 100 billion CU in an economy producing 200 billion CU annually has a 50% debt-to-GDP ratio. If debt rises to 105 billion but nominal GDP rises to 214 billion, the ratio falls to about 49.1%. Debt increased in currency terms while its ratio to annual output fell.

Interest rates, growth, existing debt, and the primary budget balance all influence debt dynamics. Currency denomination, maturity, refinancing access, and contingent liabilities also matter when judging the risks. A government borrowing in a currency it cannot issue faces constraints different from a sovereign issuing its own currency. Own-currency borrowing still faces real-resource, inflation, credibility, and institutional constraints. IMF: The fiscal and financial risks of a high-debt, slow-growth world

Original simplified path: start with 100 billion CU of debt and 200 billion CU of GDP. At 4% average interest, interest costs 4 billion. With a 1 billion primary surplus, borrowing adds a net 3 billion, producing debt of 103 billion. If nominal GDP grows 5% to 210 billion, debt-to-GDP becomes approximately 49.0%. Changing growth, interest, or the primary balance changes the path; an apparently comfortable ratio today is not a permanent guarantee.

4.7 Misconceptions to clear from the map

  • “The government and central bank do the same job.” Their instruments, decisions, and legal responsibilities differ, even when policies interact.
  • “A rate cut guarantees more jobs or higher asset prices.” Financing terms, expectations, demand, supply, and risk determine the response.
  • “A smaller inflation rate means prices returned to their old level.” If a basket rises from 100 to 108 and then by 3%, it becomes 111.24. Prices are still rising, more slowly.
  • “One debt ratio tells us whether a government is safe.” Payment timing, interest costs, currency, revenue, and future conditions are necessary context.

4.8 Check yourself

Question 1. Revenue is 500 million CU, non-interest spending is 530 million, and interest is 20 million. What are the primary and overall deficits?

Reveal answer

Answer. The primary deficit is 30 million CU; the overall deficit is 50 million CU. Outstanding debt is a separate stock.

Question 2. A policy rate falls, but your loan is fixed for another three years. Must your required payment immediately fall?

Reveal answer

Answer. No. Contract terms govern your payment. New loan offers, refinancing options, and other economic conditions may change, but a policy announcement does not rewrite an existing fixed-rate agreement.

Question 3. A government's debt rises from 80 to 84 billion CU while GDP rises from 100 to 108 billion. Did its debt-to-GDP ratio rise?

Reveal answer

Answer. No. It fell from 80% to about 77.8%. This answers an arithmetic question, not the complete sustainability question.

What you should be able to explain

By the end, you should be able to distinguish ownership from lending, financing from trading, price from cash flow, and a fund from its underlying assets. You should also be able to calculate a simple bond price and explain why good business results can accompany a falling share price.

Lesson 5.1 — Identify the claim before looking at the price

An asset is something you own that can provide economic benefits. Those benefits might be spending power, income, productive use, housing services, or a future sale. Start with the underlying object or legal claim.

This gives you a useful opening question: Who, if anyone, is obliged to pay me, and under what conditions? A company's bond, its ordinary share, and a token with its name can have very different answers. For a digital asset, inspect the specific rights and structure rather than assuming that “token” implies ownership or income.

Lesson 5.2 — Separate financing from trading

In the primary market, an issuer sells newly issued securities and receives the proceeds. In the secondary market, existing securities change hands between owners. SEC: Primary Market, SEBI: Securities Markets

Hypothetical example: River Tools issues 100,000 new shares at $10 each. Before issuance costs, it raises $1 million. Later, Alex buys 100 of those shares from Sam for $12 each. The $1,200 goes to Sam, while ownership moves to Alex.

Secondary markets still affect businesses. Investors may accept a new security more readily when they expect to be able to resell it. Market prices also influence future financing, acquisitions, and the value of share-based compensation.

Market capitalization = share price × shares outstanding. If a business has 100,000 shares priced at $20, its equity market capitalization is $2 million. That is a market valuation of equity, not a bank balance or the cash investors have collectively deposited into the company. A change in the price used to value all shares can create a large change in market capitalization without all shares trading.

Lesson 5.3 — A bond connects promised payments to today's price

A bond contract specifies payment terms. Face value is the amount due at maturity; the coupon is its interest payment. Yield describes the return implied by the price and payments. A bond's market price and its yield move in opposite directions when the promised cash flows remain unchanged. Credit quality and liquidity also matter. SEC: Corporate Bonds

Hypothetical calculation: A bond has exactly one year left. It will pay a $50 coupon and repay $1,000 at that year's end. Assume full payment, no fees, and one annual discount rate appropriate to this bond's risk.

Price today = $1,050 ÷ (1 + required yield).

Required annual yield Calculated price today Payment in one year
2% $1,029.41 $1,050
5% $1,000.00 $1,050
10% $954.55 $1,050

The contract still promises the same dollars. Buyers require a lower purchase price when they demand a higher return. The $50 coupon is 5% of face value in every row; it does not become a 10% coupon when the price falls.

Holding to maturity avoids selling at an intermediate market price, but the issuer must still pay. Inflation can reduce the purchasing power of the repayment. A bond fund generally holds and trades multiple bonds; it does not necessarily give its investors one fixed repayment date.

Lesson 5.4 — A company and its share price can move differently

Hypothetical example: Bright Machines has 100,000 shares and annual net profit of $100,000. Earnings per share, or EPS, are $1.00. At a $20 share price, its price-to-earnings ratio, or P/E, is 20.

Suppose investors had expected next year's EPS to reach $1.30. The company instead reports $1.10: profit grew 10%, but fell short of those expectations. Investors also become less confident about later growth. For illustration, suppose the price they accept becomes 17 times reported EPS:

Illustrative price = $1.10 × 17 = $18.70.

Profit rose while price fell. This example uses an assumed valuation multiple to illustrate the mechanism; P/E is not a machine that determines the next market price. EPS also measures accounting profit, which can differ substantially from cash available for distribution.

For a broader valuation framework, ask about expected future cash available to owners, its timing, and the return demanded for uncertainty. More expected cash generally supports a higher valuation, other things equal. A higher required return generally lowers today's valuation of unchanged future cash. Long-dated expectations can therefore be sensitive to changes in discount rates.

The crucial comparison is new information versus what the price already implied. A widely anticipated recovery may be reflected in prices before published economic data improve.

Lesson 5.5 — Funds package exposure; they do not erase it

A fund pools investors' money into a portfolio. An ETF trades on an exchange, so its trading price can differ from the value of its holdings per share. Its expenses reduce investor returns. SEC: Exchange-Traded Funds

An index is a rule-based measure of a group of assets; an index fund is a product attempting to follow such a measure. Neither “fund” nor “index” tells you how diversified the exposure is. A narrowly focused fund can remain heavily exposed to one industry. Diversification reduces some concentration risks but cannot prevent broad market losses. SEC: Asset Allocation and Diversification

Hypothetical example: Two funds have different names, but each holds mostly the same large technology companies. Buying both increases the number of products in your account while adding little economic variety. Read the holdings and weights to see which underlying risks repeat.

Lesson 5.6 — Measure return and risk in useful units

Hypothetical total return: You buy an asset for $100, receive $3 of income, and later sell for $104. Before fees and taxes:

Total return = ($104 − $100 + $3) ÷ $100 = 7%.

If prices for your consumption basket rose 4% over the same period, the purchasing-power return is approximately:

Real return = 1.07 ÷ 1.04 − 1 = 2.88%.

Risk includes inability to get money out when needed, business failure, changing prices, and loss of purchasing power. A quiet price chart does not establish that an asset is easy to sell or that its issuer can meet obligations. SEC: What Is Risk?

Hypothetical leverage: You use $2,000 of your money and borrow $8,000 to buy a $10,000 asset. If its value falls 10%, you have a $9,000 asset against an $8,000 debt. Your equity falls from $2,000 to $1,000: a 50% loss before interest and costs. Debt magnifies changes in the owner's remaining claim. Repayment or collateral requirements can also force a sale before the owner would choose one.

Connections to the rest of the map

  • Higher required bond yields → lower prices for unchanged fixed payments. Longer payment horizons often increase sensitivity; changing default risk can add another effect.
  • Higher business financing costs → some projects become less attractive → possible changes in investment and hiring. Existing fixed-rate borrowing may delay the effect.
  • Stronger household demand → possible revenue growth → possible profit growth. Costs and competitive pricing determine how much reaches owners.
  • Falling asset prices → weaker collateral or household wealth → possible reductions in borrowing and spending. The effect depends on ownership, debt, and financing rules.

These are channels to investigate. They are conditional relationships, not promises about tomorrow's prices.

Misconceptions to clear from the map

  • A profitable company can have an expensive share price; company quality and purchase valuation are separate questions.
  • A bond's stated coupon is not necessarily the buyer's yield.
  • A $5 share is not automatically cheaper than a $100 share; share count and business value matter.
  • Gold does not pay a contractual dividend or coupon simply because its price rises.
  • A high expected return is compensation someone demands for uncertainty, not a guaranteed outcome.

Currency-cost explorer

The question: How can a decision in another country affect your job, shopping bill, and financial assets?

The national economy is connected to other economies through goods, services, income, investment, and borrowing. Currencies translate transactions between them. On your map, borders contain many routes rather than one gate.

What you should be able to explain

By the end, you should be able to read an exchange-rate quotation, calculate an import's local-currency cost, identify who benefits from a currency movement, and distinguish trade flows from financial flows. You should also understand a simple currency hedge and why foreign-currency debt can become harder to repay.

Lesson 6.1 — Trade reallocates resources and changes people's opportunities

Trade lets people specialize and exchange. Comparative advantage concerns the opportunity cost of producing one thing instead of another; it differs from being the most productive at everything. Aggregate gains can coexist with concentrated losses for particular workers and firms. IMF: International Trade

Hypothetical illustration: Using one worker-day, Country A can make either 12 shirts or 6 bags of rice. Country B can make either 4 shirts or 4 bags of rice. Assume these production choices scale proportionally and transport is free.

Country Cost of producing one bag of rice Cost of producing one shirt
A 2 shirts forgone 0.5 bag of rice forgone
B 1 shirt forgone 1 bag of rice forgone

A is more productive in both activities, but gives up less rice when making shirts. B gives up fewer shirts when making rice. At an exchange of 3 shirts for 2 bags of rice, A obtains rice at 1.5 shirts per bag instead of its domestic cost of 2. B obtains shirts at two-thirds of a bag each instead of its domestic cost of 1.

This simplified exercise shows a source of gains from exchange. Real trade also involves transport, environmental costs, security concerns, market power, adjustment costs, and uneven bargaining power. Cheaper goods and an expanding export business can benefit some households while import competition reduces employment elsewhere. Follow both sides.

Lesson 6.2 — Goods and financial claims travel on different routes

An export sells a good or service to a nonresident. An import buys one from a nonresident. A trade balance compares exports and imports; always check whether a headline includes services or only goods.

The current account also includes cross-border income and transfers. Its balance corresponds to national saving minus investment under national accounting definitions. A deficit therefore has a financial counterpart; it is not simply a score showing who won at trade. IMF: Current Account Deficits

Hypothetical simplified economy: National saving is $80 billion and domestic investment is $100 billion. The current-account balance is −$20 billion. That financing gap can be met through increases in foreign claims on the country or reductions in its claims abroad, including relevant reserve transactions. The accounting identity does not tell you whether the resulting arrangement is wise.

Foreign financing might support productive equipment with future earnings, or spending that produces little repayment capacity. Ownership investment, long-term local-currency debt, and short-term foreign-currency debt have different risk profiles. Ask what was financed, who bears losses, in which currency, and when obligations fall due.

Lesson 6.3 — Read a currency quotation carefully

An exchange rate is the price of one currency in another. In a floating system, trading demand and supply influence that price. Different countries use different arrangements, including managed or fixed rates. Bank of England: Who Sets Exchange Rates?

Hypothetical quotation: $1 = 7 local-currency units, abbreviated LCU. This is an invented rate, not a current quote for any country.

If the rate becomes $1 = 7.7 LCU, each dollar costs more local money: the local currency has depreciated against the dollar. If it becomes $1 = 6.3 LCU, the local currency has appreciated. Always state which currency strengthened against which other currency.

Hypothetical import calculation: A machine priced at $1,000 costs 7,000 LCU at the original rate. At 7.7 LCU per dollar, it costs 7,700 LCU, a 10% increase, assuming the dollar price is unchanged. A household with 7,000 LCU can now buy about $909.09 instead of $1,000.

The inverse quotation gives a different percentage: a unit of local currency loses about 9.09% of its dollar value. Percentage changes in reciprocal quotations need not match.

An exporter receiving a fixed $1,000 now receives 7,700 LCU rather than 7,000. Its margin may improve if local costs are unchanged. Imported components, dollar debts, or a customer's demand for a lower dollar price can reduce that benefit.

Lesson 6.4 — Trace the invoice, costs, and debt

Currency movements do not pass immediately and completely into shop prices. Existing contracts, hedging, invoicing currency, inventory, and profit margins affect the timing and size of transmission. Bank of England: Monetary Policy Transmission

The dollar connects economies through trade invoices and financing even when the United States is not one of the trading partners. Dollar pricing can make the dollar exchange rate relevant to an import from a different country. Foreign-currency liabilities also change value relative to domestic income when currencies move. IMF staff analysis: Dominant Currencies and External Adjustment

Hypothetical debt example: A business earns LCU and owes $100,000. Its principal is worth 700,000 LCU at 7 LCU per dollar and 770,000 LCU at 7.7. Local-currency repayment needs rise by 70,000 LCU although the dollar principal is unchanged. Dollar export revenue could offset part of this exposure; local sales alone do not.

To analyze a firm, mark the currencies of sales, expenses, assets, and liabilities. The country where it is headquartered does not answer these questions.

Lesson 6.5 — Hedging exchanges an uncertain rate for a contractual rate

A foreign-exchange forward agrees an exchange of currencies at a specified rate and future date. It can make an expected payment or receipt more predictable. US International Trade Administration: Foreign Exchange Risk

Hypothetical importer: A business must pay $10,000 in three months. It contracts to buy those dollars then for 7.10 LCU each. Its contracted currency cost is 71,000 LCU, assuming performance by both parties and ignoring fees or collateral.

Spot rate when payment is due Cost without a hedge Contracted forward cost
7.70 LCU per dollar 77,000 LCU 71,000 LCU
6.50 LCU per dollar 65,000 LCU 71,000 LCU

The hedge protects against the first outcome but gives up the cheaper purchase available in the second. Its purpose here is budget certainty. It creates contractual obligations and can leave residual exposure if the purchase amount or timing changes. A forward rate reflects contractual pricing conditions; treat it separately from a forecast of the future spot rate.

Lesson 6.6 — Domestic purchasing power and foreign purchasing power differ

A nominal exchange rate translates currencies. A real exchange rate also takes relative prices into account. A currency can remain unchanged against another while domestic inflation changes how expensive its country's goods are relative to foreign goods. IMF: Real Exchange Rates

Hypothetical illustration: The currency quotation is unchanged. A domestic product's price rises from 100 to 110 LCU while a comparable foreign product's dollar price stays constant. The domestic product becomes 10% more expensive relative to that foreign product. Nominal exchange-rate stability did not preserve its relative price competitiveness.

Connections to the rest of the map

  • Foreign demand falls → an exporter's orders may fall → production, wages, or hiring may weaken. Effects depend on the firm's customer mix.
  • Oil's dollar price rises → import costs may rise → transport and production costs may rise. Currency appreciation, contracts, taxes, and margins can offset part of the increase.
  • Domestic interest rates rise relative to foreign rates → domestic assets may become more attractive → currency demand may increase. Expectations and perceived risk can overwhelm this channel.
  • Local currency depreciates → local cost of foreign-currency debt rises → some borrowers cut spending or seek more financing. Matching foreign-currency revenue can reduce exposure.

Misconceptions to clear from the map

  • Currency appreciation helps some buyers and hurts some sellers; it is not a universal measure of national success.
  • Imports provide goods and services, including inputs for exporters; their value is not automatically an economic loss.
  • A trade surplus and a government budget surplus describe different accounts.
  • Higher interest rates do not guarantee currency appreciation.
  • A weakening currency does not guarantee rising export volumes; invoices, capacity, imported inputs, and demand matter.

Map position: The region where production, credit, policy, markets, and global connections meet.

Mission: Explain how an ordinary slowdown can become a financial crisis, and why the same headline can lead to several different outcomes.

Learning objectives

  • Separate the level of economic activity from its growth rate, and distinguish the business cycle from longer-term productive capacity.
  • Read inflation correctly: identify the price level, inflation, disinflation, and deflation.
  • Distinguish demand shocks from supply shocks before discussing policy.
  • Calculate how debt magnifies a change in asset value and recognize refinancing risk.
  • Tell a shortage of immediately available cash from an underlying inability to repay obligations.
  • Trace reinforcing and stabilizing feedback loops without pretending that cycles follow a fixed timetable.

7.1 — The economy has several clocks

In an exploration game, a damaged power station might reduce current output while an unfinished technology upgrade changes what the base will produce later. The economy also has current activity and future productive capacity. These can move differently.

The business cycle describes broad expansions and contractions in economic activity. Output, employment, income, and sales provide different views of the same terrain. A recession is a substantial and widespread contraction, rather than any disappointing number. Two successive quarters of falling real GDP are a common shortcut, but official definitions and dating practices differ by country. IMF: What Is a Recession?

Real GDP adjusts measured output for price changes. If nominal sales increase because prices rise, that does not necessarily mean the economy produces more. Read the unit and the time comparison before interpreting a chart.

Consider a hypothetical output index. It rises from 100 to 104, then to 106. Output is still increasing, but growth slows from 4% to about 1.9%. If it then falls to 105, output contracts by about 0.9%. These are three different observations: an expansion, slower growth, and a contraction. A return from 105 to 106 is growth again, but it does not recover the output that might have been produced on a stronger path.

Longer-term capacity depends on workers, skills, equipment, institutions, and technology. A recession can leave lasting damage if investment is cancelled, viable firms disappear, or workers lose opportunities to develop skills. A recovery therefore need not return an economy to its earlier projected path. A new positive growth rate is different from making up all the lost output. IMF: The Economic Scars of Crises and Recessions

The financial cycle concerns movements in credit, leverage, asset valuations, and appetite for risk. It can overlap with the business cycle without matching it. Credit and property prices may reinforce one another: more borrowing funds purchases; higher collateral values can support further borrowing. These relationships are useful mechanisms, not a countdown to the next crash. BIS: The Financial Cycle

7.2 — Inflation measures speed; the price level measures height

The price level is an index of a basket's cost. Inflation is the percentage increase in that index over a stated period. A national consumer-price basket represents average spending, so a particular household can experience a different change in living costs. Housing, food, transport, and services have different weights for different people. Bank of England: What Is Inflation?

Use this invented basket to see the distinction:

Observation Basket cost Change from previous year Interpretation
Starting year $100.00 — Starting price level
Year 1 $110.00 +10% Inflation
Year 2 $112.20 +2% Disinflation: slower price increases
Alternative Year 2 $107.80 −2% Deflation: prices fall in this basket

Falling inflation from 10% to 2% does not restore the basket to $100. It leaves prices increasing more slowly from an already higher level. Disinflation means the inflation rate declines; deflation means the overall price level falls.

A cheaper phone can reflect better technology or stronger competition. Broad, sustained price declines accompanied by falling incomes and heavy debt can be more troubling. Fixed nominal repayments do not automatically shrink when a borrower's income falls. Ask whether falling prices reflect improved supply, weak demand, or both. IMF: Deflation — Determinants, Risks, and Policy Options

7.3 — Diagnose the shock before choosing the tool

A demand shock changes willingness or ability to spend. A supply shock changes the economy's ability or cost to produce. Their simple short-run patterns differ. IMF: Supply and Demand

Hypothetical shock First mechanism Typical initial pressure, other things equal
Households suddenly reduce purchases Weaker demand Lower output; less upward price pressure
Businesses receive a surge in orders Stronger demand Higher output; more upward price pressure near capacity
Energy supply is disrupted Costlier or constrained production Lower output; higher prices
Reliable new technology reduces costs More productive supply Higher potential output; less upward price pressure

These are starting directions, not guaranteed forecasts. Inventories, spare capacity, contracts, expectations, and international trade can modify them. A surge in orders may increase output substantially when factories have idle capacity. The same orders may mostly increase prices when production is already constrained.

Several shocks can occur together. Expensive energy can reduce supply and household purchasing power. A government transfer may support demand at the same time. Inflation can then rise while growth slows. It is a mistake to force this mixed situation into a single story such as “all inflation comes from demand.” The IMF identifies demand, supply, and expectations as distinct channels affecting inflation. IMF: Inflation — Prices on the Rise

7.4 — Leverage turns a small asset move into a large equity move

Leverage means using debt or other contractual exposure to increase the scale of assets or risks relative to one's own capital. For this simple balance sheet, equity equals assets minus debt.

Suppose a buyer purchases a $100 asset using $20 of personal funds and an $80 loan. Ignore interest, fees, taxes, and principal repayments. Debt stays fixed, and the asset is valued at the stated market price.

Asset value Debt owed Equity: assets minus debt Change in initial equity
$100 $80 $20 0%
$110 $80 $30 +50%
$90 $80 $10 −50%
$80 $80 $0 −100%
$75 $80 −$5 Negative equity

A 10% asset increase produces a 50% increase in equity; a 10% decline halves it. The starting asset-to-equity ratio is 5:1. In this simplified example, the percentage change in equity is five times the asset's percentage change. Actual loan terms, limited liability, collateral rules, and insolvency law determine what happens after a loss.

Negative equity and missed payments are different problems. An owner can have positive equity but insufficient cash to make today's payment. Someone with negative equity might continue servicing a loan from stable income. The second situation is still vulnerable, particularly if refinancing or selling becomes necessary.

Debt has another clock: maturity. An $80 loan may require only modest payments this year but full repayment next year. If replacement financing disappears, a borrower can face a crisis even before the asset's underlying income changes dramatically. Check the interest rate, repayment schedule, currency, collateral, and whether refinancing is required.

7.5 — Follow the feedback loop

Imagine a fictional property market. Buyers expect prices to rise, lenders accept optimistic valuations, and financing becomes easier. More financed purchases raise prices. Higher collateral values support larger loans. Developers build, construction workers earn income, and local shops benefit. This is a possible expansion loop.

Now suppose sales weaken. Owners and lenders reduce valuations. Some borrowers cannot refinance. Forced sales push prices lower, weakening collateral further. Lenders become cautious; developers cancel projects; workers spend less; shops receive fewer customers. A financial weakness has reached the real economy.

Expansion loop: easier credit → more purchases → higher collateral value → room for more credit.

Stress loop: lower prices → weaker borrower balance sheets → tighter credit or forced sales → weaker spending and further price pressure.

The mechanism is reinforced when many participants rely on similar collateral and refinancing sources. It is less powerful when borrowers have larger equity buffers, stable long-term funding, and income that can service debt. This fictional illustration connects credit supply, housing, banks, and firms; a BIS working paper models those interacting sectors in studying a boom and bust. BIS: Credit Supply Driven Boom-Bust Cycles

To investigate a boom, ask: What supports the payments? Rental income, wages, business profits, and the hope of selling to the next buyer have different implications. A rising market price alone does not answer that question.

7.6 — Liquidity and solvency are different repair jobs

Liquidity concerns meeting obligations when payment is due. Solvency concerns whether assets and income prospects adequately support obligations. Bank capital is a loss-absorbing source of funding; it is not the same thing as cash kept in a vault. Bank of England: Bank Capital and Liquidity

Take an invented bank with $120 of assets, $110 of liabilities, and $10 of equity. Of the assets, $10 is immediately available cash and $110 is loans that are expected to be repaid over time. If customers request $25 immediately, the bank has a $15 cash gap. That illustrates a liquidity problem, assuming the loans retain their stated value. If loan losses instead reduce total assets to $105, liabilities of $110 exceed assets: equity is negative. That illustrates a solvency problem.

Real asset values under stress are uncertain. A bank forced to sell loans quickly may obtain a lower price, so a liquidity problem can damage solvency. Conversely, doubts about solvency can cause funding to disappear and create a liquidity problem. The distinction matters even when the problems interact.

A bank run happens when withdrawals arrive faster than a bank can accommodate. Depositors' fear can become contagious, disrupting payments and credit across institutions. Regulation, deposit protection, and requirements for liquid assets and capital seek to reduce this vulnerability. The exact protections vary across jurisdictions. Bank of England: Why Do We Regulate Banks?

7.7 — Policy can cushion a shock, but cannot erase every cost

Monetary policy influences interest rates and broader financing conditions, which affect spending and employment. Its effects depend on how lenders and borrowers respond. A lower policy rate cannot ensure that a worried household borrows or that a bank finances a weak borrower. Federal Reserve: Monetary Policy

An adverse supply shock creates a harder choice. Raising rates may limit wider inflation pressures but can also weaken activity. Lower rates may support spending but risk additional inflation pressure when supply is constrained. Policy assessments therefore consider the shock's persistence and inflation expectations, not just today's headline. Federal Reserve: Navigating Economic Shocks

Fiscal policy changes taxes, transfers, and public spending. Automatic stabilizers, such as income-linked taxes and unemployment support, can cushion a slowdown under existing rules. New discretionary measures require design and implementation. Their effects depend on available capacity, targeting, financing, and the ability to change the program as conditions evolve. IMF: Fiscal Policy — Taking and Giving Away

Financial-stability tools address a different repair problem: keeping essential payments and credit functioning while losses are recognized. Providing temporary liquidity does not by itself replace missing capital. Bank resolution can reorganize a failing institution and allocate losses while protecting critical services, under the rules of the jurisdiction. Bank of England: The Approach to Resolution

7.8 — Read the dashboard without inventing a timetable

Use a set of questions rather than a single magic indicator:

  • Activity: Are output, employment, sales, and investment strengthening together?
  • Prices: Is inflation broad or concentrated? Are prices accelerating or merely high?
  • Debt: Are repayments rising relative to income? Who must refinance soon?
  • Funding: Are lenders tightening terms? Can essential borrowers still obtain finance?
  • Balance sheets: Who bears falling collateral values or currency mismatches?
  • Policy: Which problem is each tool addressing, and how long might transmission take?

Credit growth, asset prices, and unemployment can be causes, consequences, or both. Data are revised; sectors move at different speeds; and even professional forecasts miss turning points. The map improves diagnosis, but it does not supply a reliable date for the next recession. IMF: What Is a Recession?

A Mixed Shock Across the Map

Test several pathways through one fictional economy.

Mission: Connect Modules 3–7 using a fictional economy. All amounts, rates, entities, and changes below are invented for learning. The scenario is not a forecast or a description of current markets.

The starting position

Harborland uses the harbor dollar, abbreviated H$, and imports much of its energy. A manufacturer earns revenue mostly in H$ but buys imported fuel in US dollars. Its household customers have a mixture of fixed-rate and variable-rate loans. Local banks finance businesses and housing; some firms also borrow directly in US dollars. The government runs public services, and the central bank manages monetary conditions under Harborland's hypothetical framework.

Now three events arrive together: the US-dollar price of imported energy increases by 20%; the exchange rate moves from H$5 to H$6 per US dollar; and households reduce discretionary spending because they feel less secure about employment.

Do not jump directly from these events to “stocks fall” or “rates rise.” Travel through the mechanisms, recording the conditions at every junction.

Junction 1 — Real resources and international prices

Suppose imported fuel initially costs US$100 per unit. At H$5 per dollar, its local-currency cost is H$500. After the energy price rises to US$120 and the exchange rate moves to H$6 per dollar, the cost becomes H$720: 44% more, because 1.20 × 1.20 = 1.44.

This is also a lesson about quotation. One US dollar now buys 20% more H$. The H$ has fallen from US$0.20 to about US$0.1667, a decline of approximately 16.7% in its US-dollar value. State the quotation before saying that a currency moved by a percentage.

If the manufacturer cannot substitute inputs or improve efficiency quickly, its cost pressure rises. It may increase prices, accept lower margins, reduce output, or combine these responses. Competition and demand determine how much it can pass on. These are conditional inferences from the invented scenario. International invoicing and borrowing currencies affect how exchange rates reach the economy. IMF: Dominant Currencies and External Adjustment

Junction 2 — Money, credit, and repayment

A firm owes US$1 million without a currency hedge. The H$ value of that unchanged principal increases from H$5 million to H$6 million. If revenues remain in H$, the mismatch becomes harder to manage. A firm earning sufficient US-dollar revenue could have an offsetting natural hedge; the exchange-rate move alone does not tell us its net exposure.

Separately, assume a household owes H$200,000 on a variable-rate, interest-only loan. A rate change from 4% to 6% raises annual interest from H$8,000 to H$12,000, or about H$333 more each month. Principal is unchanged. A fixed-rate household need not experience the same immediate change, although refinancing later may expose it to new rates.

Borrowers may reduce spending to meet repayments. If defaults rise, lenders may tighten access to credit. The severity depends on income buffers, loan terms, and lenders' ability to absorb losses. Compare repayment cash flows before treating all borrowers as equally vulnerable.

Junction 3 — Government and central-bank choices

Harborland's central bank sees energy-driven price pressure and weaker private demand. It must assess whether inflation will spread and persist, how expectations are changing, and how fragile financing conditions have become. A rate increase may restrain spending; it cannot directly produce missing fuel. A cut may ease borrowing costs, but its inflation consequences depend on capacity and expectations. Monetary policy works through financial conditions and behavior. Federal Reserve: Monetary Policy

The government could provide targeted household support or invest in energy reliability. Support helps recipients' budgets but requires funding and may add demand. Investment may improve future capacity while taking time to deliver. Compare relief now with capacity later. Fiscal choices should be judged by their design and financing, not simply by the size of the announcement. IMF: Fiscal Policy

Junction 4 — Assets and expectations

The manufacturer's expected profits might weaken through higher costs and reduced orders. Investors might require more compensation for uncertainty. Those two changes would put downward pressure on its valuation, other things equal. Yet a competitor with cheaper domestic energy could benefit, while an exporter earning US dollars might gain local-currency revenue.

For an existing fixed-coupon bond, a higher required market yield lowers its price if promised cash flows stay unchanged. But weaker demand might instead lead investors to expect future rate cuts. Credit concerns can also change the yield on a corporate bond differently from that on a government bond. There is no single asset-market outcome implied by the shock. SEC: Fixed-Rate Bonds and Interest Rates

Junction 5 — Choose a branch, then test it

Branch A: Contained disruption. Energy supply recovers, borrowers maintain payments, and confidence stabilizes. Inflation pressure eases while spending and production improve. Check input prices, credit losses, and new orders before accepting this branch.

Branch B: Persistent cost pressure. Energy remains scarce and inflation spreads while output weakens. Policy tradeoffs become harder. Check the breadth of inflation, wages, and expectations.

Branch C: Financial amplification. Refinancing failures force asset sales, collateral weakens, credit tightens, and spending falls further. Check debt maturities, cash flows, and bank loss-absorbing capacity.

The branches are analytical possibilities, not probabilities. Your completed mission is a defensible explanation: who is exposed, what changes first, which conditions transmit the shock, and what evidence would change your view.

Field Practice & Study Plan

Build a repeatable way to read economic news.

Use five questions for every headline

  1. What changed? Identify the variable, its units, and whether it is a level, a growth rate, a flow, or a stock.
  2. Who is affected first? Name the household, business, bank, government, or overseas counterpart.
  3. Through which channel? Follow prices, income, credit, balance sheets, trade, or expectations.
  4. What conditions matter? Ask about timing, spare capacity, debt terms, currency exposure, market expectations, and policy responses.
  5. What evidence would change the explanation? Look for data that distinguish competing mechanisms.

"Interest rates fell, so stocks must rise" skips several links. Which rates fell? Was the change expected? Did expected profits fall at the same time? Did risk premiums rise? A good map keeps the alternatives visible until the evidence narrows them.

These are study sessions, not fixed calendar days. Pause whenever you cannot explain a mechanism in your own words. A second pass through a worked example is more useful than memorizing a definition you cannot apply.

Your field notebook

Use this four-line format for a story you encounter: Observation → possible mechanism → alternative explanation → evidence to check. Separate what happened from why you think it happened. Write assumptions next to conclusions. Revisit the note when new information arrives.

Your Economics Glossary

Use precise meanings when the same word has several jobs.

Term Working meaning
Asset Something owned or a claim with economic value
Liability An obligation owed to another party
Equity / net worth Assets minus liabilities
Cash flow Cash received and paid over a period
Liquidity Ability to make payments, or trade an asset without a large price impact; check the context
Solvency Ability to meet obligations considering the value of assets and liabilities
Leverage Using debt or other exposure-amplifying arrangements; definitions and ratios vary
Principal The outstanding amount borrowed, excluding interest
Interest rate The price of borrowing or return on lending, expressed relative to principal and time
Real interest rate An interest rate adjusted for inflation; distinguish expected from realized inflation
Bank reserves Central-bank money held by eligible institutions, used for settlement and other purposes
Bank capital Funding and regulatory resources that can absorb losses, depending on the instrument and rules
Bond A contractual debt claim with specified terms
Equity share An ownership claim on a business
Yield A return measure implied by price and cash flows; specify which yield
Duration A measure used to describe a bond's interest-rate sensitivity
Risk premium Extra expected compensation for bearing a particular risk
Inflation An increase in a broad price level over time
Disinflation A slowing inflation rate; the price level may still rise
Deflation A decline in a broad price level
Fiscal policy Decisions about government revenue, spending, and borrowing
Monetary policy Central-bank decisions affecting monetary and financing conditions
Exchange rate The price of one currency in terms of another; always specify quotation direction
Depreciation A currency losing value relative to another, or an asset's accounting consumption; check the context
Current account Cross-border trade in goods and services plus primary and secondary income
Balance of payments A framework recording economic transactions between residents and nonresidents
Business cycle Fluctuations in economic activity around a changing longer-run path
Productivity Output relative to an input or combination of inputs
Nominal Measured in current monetary terms
Real Adjusted for prices, or referring to productive resources; check the context
GDP A measure of domestic production over a period
Opportunity cost The value of the next-best alternative forgone
Marginal Relating to one additional unit or a small change
